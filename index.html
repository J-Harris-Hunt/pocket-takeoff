Standard counter practice
  • Depth: 25 1/2 in over standard 24 in base cabinets (1 to 1 1/2 in front overhang).
  • Seating overhang: about 12 in for counter height, 15 in for bar height, with support brackets.
  • Sink and cooktop cutouts are normally not deducted. Fabricators price the full area.
  • Draw a gap for a freestanding range (usually 30 in) and a dishwasher (24 in). There is no counter there.
  • Backsplash is commonly 4 in high. Full-height backsplash runs to the wall cabinets.
  • Stone is cut from slabs, so a fabricator may charge for more than your net area. Add 10 to 15 percent as a starting point.
  • Have a fabricator or installer template and confirm every dimension before you order.

Where the material numbers come from
  • Lumber: reference design values for visually graded dimension lumber as published in the NDS Supplement and the Southern Pine grading rules (2013 and later). Southern Pine values are already by size. Other species use the NDS size factor.
  • LVL, PSL, and glulam: typical values for common products (for example LVL Fb 2,600 psi, PSL Fb 2,900 psi, glulam 24F-V4 Fb 2,400 psi). Real products differ by maker. Use the maker's own values and span tables.
  • I-joist: a rough estimate from the flange size, assuming a flange stress of 1,400 psi and E of 1.8 million psi. I-joists are proprietary. The maker's published values are the only ones that count.
  • Posts: NDS column stability method (CP) with Ke = 1.0. Sawn 4x posts use NDS dimension lumber Fc and Emin values with the size factor. PSL posts use typical 1.8E column values (Fc 2,500 psi, E 1.8 million psi).
  • Assumes dry service, normal temperatures, no incising, and a compression edge fully braced by sheathing (CL = 1).

Load terms and wood types

Load and strength terms
  • Live load: weight that comes and goes: people, furniture, stored items. Measured in pounds per square foot (psf). Homes commonly use 40 psf for living areas and decks, 30 psf for sleeping rooms, and 10 to 20 psf for attics.
  • Dead load: the permanent weight of the building itself: framing, sheathing, drywall, flooring, roofing. Often 10 psf for floors and 15 psf for roofs, more with tile, stone, or heavy roofing.
  • Snow load: the roof load from snow. It depends on where you are, and the local building department sets it.
  • psf, plf, and lb: psf spreads a load over an area. plf (pounds per linear foot) is that load gathered onto one member. lb is a single point load, such as a post or a beam end.
  • Span: the distance between supports. A clear span is measured between the faces of the supports.
  • On-center (o.c.) spacing: the distance from the center of one joist or rafter to the next, usually 12, 16, 19.2, or 24 in.
  • Tributary width or area: the part of the floor or roof a member holds up. A beam carries half the joist span on each side. A post carries the area around it, out to halfway to the next supports.
  • Bending (moment): the bending effect of a load. It is largest at mid-span, and it is what usually limits long members.
  • Shear: the force trying to slide one part of a member past the next, greatest near the supports. It usually controls short, heavily loaded members.
  • Bearing: the crushing of wood where a member sits on a support. It decides how much seat length a beam or joist needs at each end.
  • Deflection: how far a member sags under load. Limits are written as a fraction of the span: L/360 means the sag may be no more than the span divided by 360. Floors commonly use L/360, roofs L/240, and tile floors stiffer limits.
  • Axial load and buckling: a post is loaded along its length. Tall, thin posts fail by bowing sideways (buckling) well before the wood crushes, which is why height matters so much.
  • Load duration (CD): wood carries short-term loads better than permanent ones, so snow and construction loads get a small increase in allowable stress.
  • Design values (Fb, Fv, Fc, Fc⊥, E): the published allowable stresses in bending, shear, compression along the grain, and compression across the grain (bearing), plus stiffness (E). They depend on species, grade, and product.
Wood products and what they are used for
  • Dimensional lumber (2x4 to 2x12, 4x4, 4x6): sawn boards graded by species and grade (for example Douglas Fir-Larch No.2). Used for studs, joists, rafters, short headers, deck framing, and posts. Inexpensive and easy to cut, but limited in length and more prone to warping and shrinking.
  • LVL (laminated veneer lumber): thin veneers glued with the grain running lengthwise, sold in 1 3/4 in plies. Used for headers, beams, ridge beams, and flush beams. Strong, straight, and predictable. Plies are nailed or bolted together per the maker's pattern.
  • PSL (parallel strand lumber, sold as Parallam): long wood strands glued into large solid sections. Used for heavy beams, long-span headers, girders, and posts and columns. Very strong, available in thick solid widths, and often left exposed.
  • LSL (laminated strand lumber): shorter strands glued into panels and boards. Used for rim board, tall-wall studs, short headers, and window framing. Very straight, but not as strong as LVL or PSL for long spans.
  • Glulam (glued laminated timber): layers of lumber glued together, often in 1 1/2 in lams. Used for long beams, exposed beams, curved or arched members, and garage door headers. Stock beams may be cambered (built with a slight upward curve) to offset sag.
  • I-joists: top and bottom flanges (LVL or lumber) joined by an OSB or plywood web. Used for floor and roof joists over longer spans. Light, straight, and stiff. Holes are allowed only where the maker's chart allows them, and they often need web stiffeners, blocking, and squash blocks.
  • Floor trusses (open web): parallel-chord trusses built by a truss maker. Used for long floor spans with room to run ducts and pipes through the webs.
  • Roof trusses: engineered triangle frames built for each job. Used for most new roofs. Never cut or alter them. See Trusses below.
  • Rim board: LSL, OSB, or LVL boards around the edge of a floor. They close off the joist ends and carry wall loads down.

Trusses

Reference only. Trusses are designed by the truss maker for each job, and the sealed truss design drawing controls.

Common truss types
  • Fink (W): the most common house roof truss. Webs form a W. Economical for typical spans.
  • Howe and Pratt: vertical and diagonal webs. Used for longer spans and heavier loads.
  • King post and queen post: simple trusses for short spans, sheds, and garages.
  • Scissor: sloped bottom chord for vaulted ceilings. Pushes out on walls more than flat trusses, so supports matter.
  • Attic (room-in-attic): open center for a usable room. Designed for floor loads in that area.
  • Mono (single slope): one sloped top chord. Used for lean-tos, additions, and shed roofs.
  • Hip, step-down, and jack trusses: build a hip roof. Usually carried by a girder truss.
  • Girder truss: a multi-ply truss that carries other trusses. Needs specific connections and bearing.
  • Gable end: sits on an end wall, with studs for siding. Often not designed to carry load like a regular truss.
  • Floor truss (parallel chord): open webs for running ducts, pipes, and wires. Common in 12 to 24 in depths.
  • Piggyback: a cap truss set on top of a flat-top truss when the full height is too tall to ship.
Rules that apply to every truss
  • Never cut, notch, drill, or remove any chord, web, or plate. Repairs need an engineered repair detail from the truss maker.
  • Install temporary and permanent bracing as the truss drawings and the BCSI guide (SBCA and TPI) require. Most truss collapses happen during installation.
  • Do not add load the truss was not designed for: attic storage, HVAC units, solar panels, water heaters, or heavy ceilings. Ask the truss maker first.
  • Set each truss on the bearing points shown on its drawing, with the specified connectors and hurricane ties.
  • Do not stack heavy material on trusses during construction.
  • Keep a copy of the truss design drawings for the building official and future work.

How-to guides

General steps for common installs. The product maker's instructions and your local code come first. Not following them can void a warranty.

Carpet (stretch-in over pad)
  1. Remove old flooring, staples, and debris. Fix squeaks and fill low spots. The subfloor should be clean, dry, and flat.
  2. Install tack strip around the room, pins toward the wall, leaving a gap from the wall a little less than the carpet thickness (often about 3/8 in). Skip doorways.
  3. Lay pad inside the tack strip, not over it. Butt seams tight, tape them, and staple (wood) or glue (concrete) the pad down. Run pad seams across carpet seams, not under them.
  4. Plan seams away from traffic paths and doorways, with all pieces running the same pile direction. Rough-cut carpet 3 to 6 in oversize on every side.
  5. Join seams with hot-melt seam tape and a seaming iron. Press the seam closed and weight it while it cools.
  6. Hook one corner on the tack strip, then use a knee kicker along the first walls.
  7. Stretch across the room with a power stretcher. A knee kicker alone cannot stretch a full room and leads to wrinkles later.
  8. Trim the excess with a wall trimmer and tuck the edge into the gully with a stair tool.
  9. Cut vent openings, install transition strips at doorways, and vacuum.
Luxury vinyl plank (click, floating)
  1. Acclimate the boxes in the room if the maker requires it. Check the subfloor is flat to the maker's spec (often about 3/16 in over 10 ft), clean, and dry. Test concrete for moisture if required.
  2. Undercut door jambs and casing so planks slide under them.
  3. Plan the layout so the first and last rows are not thin slivers. Lay planks parallel to the longest wall or the main light source.
  4. Install underlayment only if the maker allows it (many planks have it attached).
  5. Leave the expansion gap the maker specifies (often about 1/4 in) at every wall and fixed object. Use spacers.
  6. Click rows together as the maker shows. Stagger end joints by at least the maker's minimum (often 6 to 8 in). Avoid patterns like stair steps.
  7. Cut with a utility knife (score and snap) or a saw. Use the cut-off to start the next row if it is long enough.
  8. Do not nail or screw through a floating floor, and keep heavy cabinets off it unless the maker allows.
  9. Install baseboard or quarter round to cover the gap, fastening it to the wall, not the floor. Add transitions.
Laminate (click, floating)
  1. Acclimate as the maker requires, usually 48 hours in the room. Check flatness and moisture as for LVP.
  2. On concrete, lay a 6 mil plastic vapor barrier (or a combo underlayment), lapped and taped. Then lay underlayment with seams butted and taped.
  3. Undercut door jambs. Plan the layout to avoid thin first and last rows.
  4. Keep the expansion gap the maker specifies (often 1/4 to 3/8 in) at every wall, pipe, and fixed object.
  5. Lock boards together, using a tapping block and pull bar. Never hit the edge directly.
  6. Stagger end joints by at least the maker's minimum (often 12 in).
  7. Use transitions where the maker requires breaks, such as doorways and long runs.
  8. Keep laminate out of wet areas unless it is rated water-resistant, and even then follow the maker's limits.
  9. Install trim to cover the gaps, fastened to the wall only.
Solid hardwood (nail-down)
  1. Use solid hardwood only on or above grade, over a wood subfloor, not directly on concrete.
  2. Acclimate the wood. Check moisture in both the flooring and the subfloor with a meter, and keep them within the range the maker specifies.
  3. Fix squeaks, re-screw the subfloor, and check flatness. Lay the underlayment paper or felt the maker specifies.
  4. Run boards perpendicular to the floor joists when possible.
  5. Snap a straight starting line. Leave the expansion gap the maker specifies (often about 3/4 in) at walls.
  6. Face-nail and blind-nail the first rows by hand where the flooring nailer will not fit.
  7. Use a flooring nailer with the cleats or staples the maker specifies, near each end and at the spacing it calls for (often every 6 to 8 in).
  8. Rack out boards from several boxes so colors mix and end joints stagger well.
  9. Face-nail the last rows, fill nail holes, and install trim. Sand and finish if the flooring is unfinished.
Engineered wood (float, glue, or nail)
  1. Check which methods the maker allows for your product and floor. Many engineered floors can go on concrete and below grade. Solid hardwood cannot.
  2. Acclimate and test moisture as the maker requires. Flatten the subfloor.
  3. Floating: lay the specified underlayment and vapor barrier, then click or glue the joints. Keep the expansion gap.
  4. Glue-down: spread the maker's adhesive with the notched trowel it specifies. Work in sections within the adhesive's open time, and roll or weight the boards.
  5. Nail or staple: use the fastener size and spacing the maker gives. Thin products need fine staples, not standard flooring cleats.
  6. Stagger end joints, mix boxes, and keep the expansion gap at walls.
  7. Install trim and transitions. Keep traffic off glue-down floors as long as the adhesive requires.
Tile (thinset)
  1. Make sure the floor is stiff enough. The TCNA Handbook asks for L/360 for ceramic and porcelain and L/720 for natural stone.
  2. Prepare the substrate: cement backer board set in thinset and screwed on its pattern, or an uncoupling membrane, over a sound subfloor. Concrete must be clean and crack-free, or treated for cracks.
  3. Find the room's center lines and dry-lay tile both ways, so the edge cuts are at least half a tile where possible.
  4. Mix thinset as the bag says, let it slake, and remix. Use the trowel notch the tile and thinset makers call for. Large tiles need bigger notches and back-buttering.
  5. Comb thinset in straight lines, set each tile, and slide it slightly across the ridges. Lift one now and then to check coverage.
  6. Use spacers, keep joints straight, and use a leveling system for large tiles to control lippage.
  7. Leave movement joints at the perimeter and wherever the tile meets another surface. Fill them with flexible sealant, not grout.
  8. Let the thinset cure as the maker says, then grout. Wipe the haze, let it cure, and seal cement grout if the maker recommends it.
Hanging drywall
  1. Check that framing is straight and that blocking is in place for cabinets, grab bars, and TVs. Mark stud and joist centers on the floor and top plate.
  2. Hang ceilings first, then walls. Run sheets perpendicular to the framing.
  3. On walls, hang the top row first, tight to the ceiling. Then hang the bottom row, leaving about 1/2 in at the floor.
  4. Land every end joint on framing, and stagger end joints from row to row. Keep joints from lining up with door and window corners. Cut around openings so a sheet spans the corner.
  5. Measure each piece and cut it about 1/8 in short. Score with a utility knife against a T-square, snap, and cut the back paper.
  6. Cut outlet and fixture openings with a spiral saw or keyhole saw. Mark them before hanging so you do not miss any.
  7. Fasten with drywall screws: common spacing is about 12 in on ceilings and 16 in on walls, at least 3/8 in from edges. Set each head just below the surface without tearing the paper. Fire-rated work must follow the listed assembly's screw spacing.
  8. Install corner bead on outside corners, straight and plumb.
  9. Tape and finish: bed tape in compound on every joint, then fill and feather with at least two more coats, wider each time. Sand lightly between coats. Wear a dust mask.
